# Why Are We Here?

## My Starting Point

I am a designer rather than a programmer. In the past, I saw learning programming mainly as a way to turn my design ideas into working experiences. But as AI becomes increasingly capable of generating functional code from natural language, that reason no longer feels sufficient. If I may not need to write every line of code myself, why do I still need to understand programming?

## When I Knew What I Wanted, But Could Not Build It

During my undergraduate studies, I worked on interaction design projects for communities that are often overlooked by mainstream digital innovation, including older adults and people with visual impairments. These experiences made inclusive design an important part of my practice.

For my graduation project, I designed an interactive music mat for visually impaired users. Instead of focusing only on compensating for visual impairment, I wanted to create an enjoyable experience where visually impaired and sighted users could play together through music and body movement. My aim was to use Arduino and TouchDesigner to build a full-scale functional prototype. Stepping on different keys would trigger different piano notes, while movements such as clapping or tapping different parts of the body would trigger instrumental sounds. I also used TouchDesigner to connect these interactions with dynamic visual patterns, so that one movement could produce both sound and visual feedback.

I knew quite precisely what experience I wanted to design, but I did not know how to specify it computationally. Because I was unfamiliar with programming and the rules behind how these systems worked, it was difficult to independently turn my design intentions into a reliable working experience.

## What Changes When AI Can Write the Code?

If I developed the same project today, AI could probably generate much of the code that I struggled to write at the time. I could describe an interaction, such as asking the system to trigger a sound and a visual animation when a user claps, and AI might be able to produce a technically working solution. This makes the old reason for learning programming — simply being able to write the code myself — less convincing to me. 

This question is not entirely new. In her notes on Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine, Ada Lovelace argued that the machine could perform what humans knew how to instruct it to perform, rather than originate intentions of its own. Although today's AI is very different from the Analytical Engine, this distinction still makes me think about where intention sits in my own design process.

More than a century later, Alan Turing approached the relationship between humans and machines from another direction. In Computing Machinery and Intelligence, he replaced the abstract question “Can machines think?” with the imitation game, shifting attention towards what a machine can actually demonstrate through its behaviour. In 2026, when AI can already produce code from ordinary language, this history makes the question feel more immediate to me: if a machine can produce the result I ask for, what understanding do I still need as the designer?

However, working code is not necessarily a successful interaction. In my music mat project, making an action trigger a sound was only the beginning. I still needed to decide whether the sound, timing, visual movement, and relationship between different forms of feedback created the sensory experience I intended. These decisions depended on my design intentions and judgement, especially when the aim was to create an experience in which visually impaired and sighted users could participate together.

This is why AI changes, rather than removes, my reason for learning programming. I may not need to write every line of code myself, but I need enough understanding to specify what I want, read what has been generated, question how it works, and modify it when the result does not match my intention. I can outsource some coding to AI, but I should not outsource my judgement with it.

## Code as a Creative Medium

Dylan Beattie's *The Art of Code* also changed the way I think about programming. His examples show that code can be more than a practical tool for making software work; it can also become a creative and expressive medium. This idea feels particularly relevant to me as a designer.

In design, understanding a material is not only about knowing how to manufacture something with it. Its properties and limitations also influence what a designer can imagine and create. I am beginning to see programming in a similar way. If computation is becoming part of the material I design with, relying entirely on AI to manipulate that material would leave part of my own design process inaccessible to me.

Don Ihde's account of human–technology relations gives me another way to understand this. Rather than treating technologies as neutral tools, Ihde describes how they mediate the relationship between people and the world. For me, this suggests that learning programming is also a way of understanding how a computational medium shapes what I can perceive, make, and imagine as a designer.

This does not mean that I need to reject AI or become a programmer who writes everything from scratch. I would rather treat AI as a collaborator that can help me work with code more efficiently. But learning programming gives me a better understanding of the medium behind its outputs. That understanding can help me recognise possibilities, question limitations, and make more deliberate creative decisions instead of simply accepting whatever the system produces.

## Why I Am Here

I do not know how much of the code I will need to write myself in the future. As AI continues to improve, perhaps less and less. But this does not make learning programming meaningless to me. It changes what I want to learn from it.

I am not here to compete with AI at producing code faster. I am here because computation is becoming part of the material through which I design. I want to understand it well enough to specify what I mean, recognise when something is wrong, explore possibilities I might otherwise miss, and make my own decisions about the experiences I create. AI can help me produce an outcome, but I still want to understand and take responsibility for the choices behind it. For me, that is what survives even if the way we write code changes beyond recognition.

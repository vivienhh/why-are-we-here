# Why Are We Here?

## My Starting Point

As a student with a background in design, I used to see programming mainly as a way to turn my ideas into experiences that could actually work. But as AI becomes increasingly capable of generating functional code from natural language, that reason no longer feels sufficient. If I may no longer need to write every line of code myself, why do I still need to understand programming?

## When I Knew What I Wanted, But Could Not Build It

During my undergraduate studies, I worked on interaction design projects for people who are often overlooked in mainstream digital innovation, including older adults and people with visual impairments. These experiences made inclusive design an important part of my practice.

For my graduation project, I designed a music-based motion game for people with visual impairments. Through music, body movement, and multisensory feedback, I wanted to create a more accessible way for them to experience space and interact through play. Rather than simply compensating for visual impairment, I wanted visually impaired and sighted users to take part in the same musical interaction together.

I used Arduino and TouchDesigner to build a full-scale music mat prototype. Stepping on different keys triggered piano notes, while actions such as clapping or tapping different parts of the body produced different instrumental sounds. I also used TD to connect these movements with dynamic visuals, so that a single action could produce both sound and visual feedback. As users interacted, these visual elements gradually formed a graphic score, encouraging sighted users to join the experience.

I had a clear idea of the experience I wanted to create, but my limited understanding of programming made it difficult to translate that intention into a stable computational system. For example, I needed to work out when different sounds should be triggered, how sensitive the motion detection should be, and how sound and visual feedback should respond to each other. At the time, I could describe the experience I wanted, but I lacked the ability to turn that intention into specific computational logic.

## What Changes When AI Can Write the Code?

If I developed the same project today, AI could probably generate much of the code I struggled with. I could simply describe the interaction I wanted, such as producing sound and visual feedback in response to a clap, and AI might generate a working solution. This makes my old reason for learning programming feel less convincing.

Ada Lovelace argued that Babbage's Analytical Engine could perform what humans knew how to instruct it to perform, rather than originate intentions of its own. More than a century later, Alan Turing's Computing Machinery and Intelligence shifted attention from “Can machines think?” towards what a machine can demonstrate through its behaviour. These ideas make me question where intention sits in my own design process: if a machine can produce the result I ask for, what do I still need to understand as the designer?

Of course, working code does not necessarily mean a successful interaction. Even if AI could help me program this game, making an action successfully trigger sound and visual feedback would still only be the first step. I would still need to judge whether the sound felt right, whether the timing was appropriate, how the visuals should change, and whether these different forms of feedback came together to create the sensory experience I intended. Especially when designing for visually impaired and sighted users together, these decisions would still depend on my own design intentions and judgement.

This is why, for me, AI changes my reason for learning programming rather than removing it. I may not need to write every line of code myself, but I still need enough understanding of programming to specify what I want, read what AI generates, understand how it works, and modify it when the result does not match my intention. I can hand over some of the coding to AI, but I should not hand over my judgement with it.

## Code as a Creative Medium

Dylan Beattie's The Art of Code changed the way I think about programming. His examples made me realise that code can be more than a practical tool; it can also be a creative and expressive medium.

In design, understanding a material is not simply about knowing how to use it. Its properties and limitations also shape what a designer can imagine and create. If computation is one of the “materials” I design with, relying entirely on AI would leave part of my own design process inaccessible to me.

Don Ihde's discussion of human–technology relations gives me another way to think about this. Ihde focuses on how technologies mediate the relationship between people and the world. For me, learning programming is also a way of understanding how computational media shape what I can perceive, create, and imagine as a designer.

This does not mean that I need to reject AI. I would rather use it to work with code more efficiently and bring my ideas to life. But learning programming allows me to understand code as a creative medium, discover new possibilities and limitations, and make more deliberate creative decisions rather than simply accepting the results AI gives me.

## Why I Am Here

As AI continues to develop, I may need to write less code myself. But this does not make learning programming meaningless to me. Instead, it changes what I want to learn from it.

I am not learning programming to compete with AI over who can write code faster. What matters to me is understanding how my design intentions are translated into computational logic, judging whether something that technically works creates the experience I want, and exploring possibilities beyond the answers AI gives me. AI can help me reach a result more quickly, but what I want to create, which results I choose to accept or change, and why I make those choices are still decisions I need to make for myself.

The way we write code may change dramatically in the future, but I do not want increasingly powerful tools to gradually take away my ability to understand, judge, choose, and create. For me, that is why learning programming still matters.
